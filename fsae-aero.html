Establishing the mesh first

Before any of the sweep results meant anything, I had to know the grid was not the thing driving them. A mesh independence study came first — refining until the solution stopped moving with cell count, so that a change in the results could be attributed to the geometry rather than to the discretization.

This is the unglamorous half of the project and the half I would defend hardest. Sixty-five runs on an unconverged mesh is sixty-five confident wrong answers.

The sweep

  • Angle of attack across the usable range of the element.
  • Element overlap — the relationship between elements, which governs how the slot flow behaves and whether the upper element stays attached.

Post-processing was done in Fluent directly — no scripting layer and no automation.

Going transient

A steady solve averages the turbulence away, and separated flow on a multi-element wing is where that averaging hides the most. So I also ran an 8.18M-cell IDDES transient on the wing mesh — a hybrid approach that models the attached boundary layer but resolves the large, unsteady structures in separated flow — as an approximately four-day solve on 16 cores.

Why the isolated wing was not enough

The isolated-wing results stopped predicting what the wing did once it was installed on the car. So I extended the study to a full-vehicle external aerodynamics model — one that resolves the wing sitting in the wake of everything ahead of it, the floor interaction underneath, and the wheel wake.

A wing that works in isolation and a wing that works at the back of a car are two different wings. Finding out which one I had designed was the point of the exercise.

Validation

A scaled wind tunnel test of the wing profile is planned, to correlate the predicted values against measured ones. Until that happens these are predictions, and I would rather label them that way than imply otherwise.
